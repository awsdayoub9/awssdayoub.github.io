Every page I have found on this question is written by a partner, and it argues for partners. That is their page; this is the other side. I take freelance Odoo projects, and I have also worked inside two companies delivering Odoo projects: Hash Code IT Solutions in Dubai, and iLines Solutions in Riyadh, where I am still employed. I have seen what a delivery team gives a client that one person cannot, and what one person gives that a team cannot. Both are real.

## Start from the shape of your company, not from the quotes

The right answer depends on the buyer more than on the vendor. Before comparing anyone, write down:

- how many legal entities and countries are in scope
- how many sites, and how many users will work in the system daily
- which apps are in scope, and whether payroll or e-invoicing is among them
- whether you have in-house IT that will administer Odoo after go-live
- whether procurement requires a company on the contract, or a service agreement with penalties
- whether hosting and licences must come from the same vendor as the implementation

Those answers decide the question; the comparison below explains why.

## The cases for an Odoo partner

Odoo runs a partner programme and publishes a directory of partners by country and industry. A partner is a company, and a company brings things an individual cannot.

Capacity in parallel. A rollout across several countries, or a programme where finance, supply chain and HR move at the same time, needs several people working at once. One person can sequence that work, not parallelise it.

Cover. A company can replace a consultant who is ill, on leave or leaving, and staff a support desk during hours one person cannot cover. If your contract needs guaranteed response times with penalties, you need an organisation behind the signature.

Large user bases. Training and supporting a very large number of users in a short window is a team job, and so is a data migration across several entities at once.

Commercial arrangements. Some buyers want hosting, licences, implementation and support from one vendor under one agreement, with an account manager separate from the people who build. That is a partner's business model, not a freelancer's.

Procurement rules. If your organisation cannot contract with an individual, the decision is already made, and there is no point pretending otherwise.

If two or more of those describe you, talk to partners. A freelancer who tells you otherwise is selling.

## The cases for a freelancer

Defined scope. One or a few entities, a known list of apps, a requirement that can be written down. Implementations of that shape are most of what small and mid-sized companies need.

One accountable person. The person who runs your requirements workshop writes the specification, builds and configures the system, tests it with your key users and hands it over. Nothing is lost between a salesperson, a consultant and a developer, because they are the same person. That is the structural advantage.

Direct access. You message the person who wrote the code.

Bounded technical work. Custom development against a clear specification, version migrations, and taking over a half-finished system are natural freelance work: one person reads everything before anyone changes anything.

Cost structure. A freelancer has no bench to keep busy, no sales team and no account layer; you pay for the work of the person doing it. Whether the total is lower depends on the scope. What changes is that the price is attached to the work rather than to the organisation around it. Neither side should quote before discovery, and a fixed price offered before anyone has seen your processes is a warning sign from either.

## The objection you will hear

Partners will ask what happens if the freelancer disappears, and it is a fair question. The answer is a set of practices rather than a promise, and it is set out on the [support page](/services/odoo-support/).

## What I learned inside delivery teams

Working inside delivery teams showed me the trade honestly. A team gives a client cover and parallel capacity. It also introduces handoffs: the consultant who gathers the requirements is often not the developer who builds them, and every handoff loses detail that someone later pays to recover. Good teams manage this with written specifications and reviews. A freelancer avoids it by not having the handoff, and pays for that with limited capacity. Neither is free; the question is which cost your company can carry more easily.

## How to de-risk either choice

The same items protect you whichever way you go.

- A written scope after discovery, with changes written up and approved before work starts.
- Code ownership: modules in a repository you own from day one, and admin access held by you, because no database is upgraded until its custom modules exist for the target version, whoever wrote them.
- A staging database, so nothing reaches production untested.
- UAT with your key users signing off against the specification, not against a demo.
- A handover pack: documentation, configuration register, credentials, and a support arrangement in writing.

My own version is on [how I work](/how-i-work/); ask any partner for theirs.

## Side by side

| Question | Partner | Freelancer |
|---|---|---|
| Who scopes and who builds | Usually different people | The same person |
| Access to the builder | Through a project manager or a ticket | Direct |
| Parallel workstreams | Yes, with a lead per stream | One stream at a time |
| Cover for absence | Another team member | A written support arrangement and your own repository |
| Contract counterparty | A company | An individual |
| Hosting and licences | Often bundled | Arranged separately, usually by you |
| Support after go-live | A desk with defined hours | A named person with agreed hours |
| Upgrade safety | Depends on the team's coding standards | Depends on one person's standards, which you can read in the repository |

## Questions to ask either of them

- Which requests would you push back on, and show me one you declined.
- Who owns the repository on day one?
- Show me a migration you ran and what broke.
- What does your handover pack contain?
- Who exactly will build my system, and can I talk to them now?
- What happens if you, or the developer assigned to us, are unavailable for an extended period?
- How do you decide what is configured and what is built, and where is that written down?

The answers tell you more than the company size does. My own engagement terms, such as pricing model, NDA and editions, are on the [FAQ page](/faq/), and my employment background is on the [about page](/about/).

## Working with both

The two are not exclusive. A freelancer can build custom modules or run a migration alongside a partner that holds hosting and licences, or alongside in-house IT. What makes it work is a written split of responsibilities before work starts, one owner per module and one shared staging database. What breaks it is two vendors and nobody accountable for the seam.

The answer for your company might be a partner. If it is, I will say so.
